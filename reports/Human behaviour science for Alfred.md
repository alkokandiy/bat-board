# Design Alfred to notice, not nag

The single most consequential finding for Alfred is that feedback is not safe by default: across **607 effect sizes and 23,663 observations**, feedback interventions improved performance on average (**d = 0.41**) but **more than a third made performance worse**, and the mechanism is where the message points — effectiveness falls as attention moves away from the task and toward the self ([Kluger & DeNisi abstract](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc)). Everything else follows from that. A companion that notices things about a person is running a feedback intervention several times a day, and the evidence says the way to make it more caring is not to add warmth but to subtract judgement: describe the board, not the person; offer options, not obligations; and send fewer, better-placed messages. Three mechanisms bat-board has already shipped — consecutive-day streaks, a points/level economy, and the act of counting someone's tasks at all — are precisely the ones the literature warns about, because a broken streak reduces subsequent behaviour **even when the break was outside the person's control** ([UDaily on Silverman & Barasch, *JCR* 2023](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)), tangible expected rewards undermine intrinsic motivation at **d = −0.28 to −0.40** ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)), and a meta-analysis of **94 studies** found electronic performance monitoring raises stress **regardless of how the monitoring is configured**, while monitoring without an explicit purpose showed **no performance gain at all** ([Ravid et al. 2022](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/2022-ravid.pdf)). The genuinely strong positive findings are narrower than productivity culture suggests but they are real and they are all implementable in Alfred's existing signal architecture: monitoring one's own goal progress causes attainment (**d+ = 0.40**, 138 studies, N = 19,951, [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)), if-then plans produce **d ≈ 0.65** on goal attainment ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.academia.edu/55735607/Implementation_intentions_Strong_effects_of_simple_plans)), and giving an unfinished goal a specific plan *eliminates* its intrusive cognitive cost without the task being done ([Masicampo & Baumeister, summarised](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2010/11/brain-clutter-whats-left-undone-lingers.html)). The contact-timing question the owner raised has a cheap, defensible answer: biological night is estimable from habitual bed and wake times alone, because dim-light melatonin onset sits roughly **2–3 hours before habitual bedtime** ([CDC circadian phase markers](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/189902/cdc_189902_DS1.pdf)), so quiet hours should be derived per user rather than fixed at 22:00–08:00. And the whole project has a safety ceiling it must respect: in the strongest companion-AI trial available (4-week RCT, n = 981 completers), **greater voluntary daily chatbot use predicted worse outcomes on every measure**, including reduced real-world socialising ([MIT Media Lab / OpenAI, arXiv 2503.17473](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17473v2)) — which means rising Alfred usage is a risk marker, not a success metric.

## Biological night, not the clock, should set the quiet window

Alfred's current quiet hours (22:00–08:00, per-user timezone) are directionally right and mechanistically wrong in one respect: they are clock-shaped, and the thing they are protecting is not. Sleep timing is governed by two interacting processes — homeostatic sleep pressure built by adenosine during waking, and a circadian process read out by melatonin onset and the core body temperature rhythm — and this two-process model has been the field's framework for three decades ([Borbély et al. 2016 reappraisal](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/the-twoprocess-model-of-sleep-regulation-a-reappraisal/)). The usable consequence is that **an app that knows only habitual bed and wake times can estimate a user's internal night without any sensor**: DLMO occurs about **2–3 hours before habitual bedtime** and about **7 hours before the core body temperature minimum** ([CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/189902/cdc_189902_DS1.pdf)). Chronotype is real and partly heritable (twin estimates **12–42%**, common-SNP heritability **13.7%**), but the genetic extremes differ in sleep timing by only about **25 minutes** ([Jones et al., bioRxiv 303941](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/303941.full.pdf)) while observed chronotypes spread over hours — so lateness is mostly environmental, and a user's own reported schedule is a far better input than any population default. The implementable rule: suppress all ordinary outbound contact from (habitual sleep onset − 1 h) to habitual wake, computed per user, and let the fixed 22:00–08:00 window exist only as the fallback for users who have told Alfred nothing.

The reason this matters more than politeness is the pathway by which a night message does harm. The direct claim that receiving notifications wrecks sleep is **contested**: one cross-sectional student study found night-time notifications were not a significant predictor of sleep quality ([PMC13408670](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13408670/)) and adolescent tracking found no relationship between notification volume and night-waking duration ([UNC Winston Center](https://winstoncenter.unc.edu/overnight-smartphone-use-can-disrupt-adolescent-sleep/)), while other work found that greater *awareness* of night notifications was significantly associated with sleep problems and worse subjective quality ([PMC10899973](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10899973/)). What is clearer is the mechanism: **picking the phone up is more disruptive than receiving the alert** — when teenagers handled their phone around a night waking they stayed awake longer, converting a brief stir into a prolonged one ([UNC Winston Center](https://winstoncenter.unc.edu/overnight-smartphone-use-can-disrupt-adolescent-sleep/)). In the ABCD-study analysis, leaving the ringer on was associated with a **25% higher risk of sleep disturbance**, **16%** had been woken in the past week by a call or text, and **19%** used a device when they woke overnight ([HealthDay report on *J Adolesc Health* 2024](https://healthday.com/health-news/sleep-disorder/screens-tweens-and-sleep-study-uncovers-tips-for-better-rest)) — though that sample is 11–12-year-olds and cross-sectional, so the figure should not be generalised to adults. The engineering consequence is **deliver-on-wake rather than deliver-at-time**: a nudge generated at 23:40 should be queued and surfaced at the user's wake, because a message merely *waiting* is enough to trigger the pickup pathway. And the quiet window should have **no exception tier**, because the alarm-fatigue literature shows a rare tier only keeps working if it stays rare.

The owner's 2am example is the interesting inverse case: the user is awake and messaging, with an early start tomorrow. Alfred should say something, and the evidence tells it exactly what register to use. Someone working at 02:00 is combining three independently documented penalties. First, they are at or near the circadian trough — occupational-safety guidance identifies **02:00–05:00** (and 13:00–15:00) as the windows where circadian rhythms induce sleep and risk peaks, with drowsy-driving crashes concentrated between midnight and 06:00 ([CDC/NIOSH](https://WWW.CDC.GOV/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod11/02.html); [KYTC drowsy driving](https://transportation.ky.gov:443/HighwaySafety/Documents/Drowsy%20UPDATE.pdf)) — so the work itself is poor-quality. Second, they are accruing a deficit whose cost is cumulative: in Van Dongen's canonical restriction study (**n = 66**, 3/5/7/9 h time in bed for 7 days), the 3-hour group's lapses **increased steadily across all seven days with no plateau**, the 5- and 7-hour groups declined and stabilised at a reduced level, and only the 9-hour group stayed at baseline ([PubMed 12603781](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12603781/)). Third, tomorrow's emotional regulation will be measurably worse: one night of deprivation produced a **~60% increase in amygdala reactivity** to emotional images with decreased medial prefrontal activity, the region that exerts top-down control ([UC Berkeley on Yoo et al. 2007](https://newsarchive.berkeley.edu/news/media/releases/2007/10/22_sleeploss.shtml)). Alfred should name a mechanism and offer to move the work; it should not moralise about discipline. It can also debunk helpfully, because the "I'm not even tired" objection has a real answer: the late-evening second wind is the **Wake Maintenance Zone**, a strong circadian arousal signal occurring before melatonin onset that opposes accumulating sleep pressure and is detectable even during 40 hours of continuous deprivation ([Shekleton et al., PMC6054682](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6054682/)). Not feeling sleepy at 23:00 is a transient circadian artefact, not evidence of not needing sleep.

Two further sleep facts earn Alfred behavioural rules rather than lectures. People are systematically bad judges of their own sleep impairment: **400 mg of caffeine six hours before bed cut objectively measured total sleep time by more than an hour while participants did not notice** ([Drake et al. 2013, PMC3805807](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807/)), which pairs with Van Dongen's subjective/objective divergence to license the single most useful sentence Alfred can own — *feeling fine is not evidence of being fine* — provided it is said once, about a specific observation, and not repeated as a theme. And after a short night Alfred should **soften rather than push**: the amygdala/mPFC finding means challenge and confrontation land differently, and sleep inertia (reported from **0 to ~35 minutes**, with one study finding ≤15 min sufficed for decrements to dissipate after 20-, 40- and 60-minute naps, [Hilditch & McHill review](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/3314e5ee-e2af-4e68-a541-99e22d7a654d)) means the first fifteen minutes after waking is the wrong time to ask for a decision. Escalate on **two or three consecutive** short nights, not one, because the evidenced harm is cumulative.

## Feedback helps on average and harms one time in three

Kluger and DeNisi's result is the spine of Alfred's wording policy, and its practical form is a single test applicable to every generated sentence: **does this point at the task or at the person?** Feedback Intervention Theory holds that feedback shifts the locus of attention among task-learning, task-motivation and meta-task (self-related) levels, and that effectiveness *decreases* as attention moves up toward the self ([Kluger & DeNisi](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc)). "You've been inconsistent lately" and "this is the third week you've slipped" are self-level messages from a system the user may already partly distrust — the worst available combination, since affective algorithm aversion is documented alongside the cognitive kind ([Technology in Society 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/teinso/v79y2024ics0160791x24002744.html)). "Two tasks are past their date: X and Y" and "last week the 6am slot worked and the 9pm one didn't" are the same information at task level. The one-third-harmful figure is also the honest justification for Alfred's **silence option**: when there is nothing task-level and actionable to say, not sending is an evidence-based choice.

Self-Determination Theory supplies the positive form. The autonomy-supportive behaviour set is specific and reads like a message template: take the other person's perspective, provide explanatory rationales, **acknowledge and accept negative affect**, rely on **informational, non-pressuring language**, offer meaningful choice, and display patience ([Reeve](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4EXRpdV7); [Reeve & Jang 2006](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/2006_ReeveJang_JEP.pdf); [Reeve & Cheon 2024](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/2024_ReeveCheon_TATE.pdf)). The effects are modest rather than transformative — a meta-analysis of **73 SDT-informed health interventions** found small-to-medium changes in SDT constructs and behaviour, described by the authors as "modest, heterogeneous" ([Ntoumanis et al.](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-self-determination-theory-informed-interventio/)) — and a preregistered experiment (N = 156) found autonomy-supportive instructions raised *perceived* autonomy with **no difference in motor performance** ([St. Germain et al. 2025](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/298)). So autonomy-supportive phrasing should be sold internally as protecting the relationship over months, not as a per-message performance lever. That is still the right trade for a companion the user has to tolerate for years.

The backfire side is where the effect sizes are sharpest. Meta-analyses of **35 studies, N = 10,658** found that high freedom-threatening language increases **anger, negative cognitions and psychological reactance** relative to low-threat language, with forceful imperatives and controlling directives as the specific trigger, and the effect moderated by **behaviour repetitiveness** ([HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/message-effects-on-psychological-reactance-meta-analyses/); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/forceful-language-makes-people-resist-health-advice/)). Reactance is an intertwined anger-plus-counter-arguing response, which matters because the anger component is not dissolved by better reasons. The repetitiveness moderator is directly aimed at a nudge engine: the habit Alfred mentions most often is exactly the case where force is most costly, so **repeated nudges about the same behaviour must get softer and rarer, never more insistent**. Shame is worse than guilt and both are poor tools — shame appeals produce **anger and perceived manipulative intent** more than guilt appeals ([Toronto Metropolitan highlight](https://www.torontomu.ca/trsm-research/achievements-impact/research-highlights/unlocking-the-power-of-guilt-and-shame--tailoring-health-message-for-better-choices)), guilt and shame appeals induce **defensive processing that inhibits persuasion** ([Duhachek, Agrawal & Han 2012](https://depts.washington.edu/aglab/docs/Duhachek,%20Agrawal%20&%20Han%20(2012)_Guilt%20versus%20Shame.pdf)), the operative distinction is behaviour (guilt) versus character (shame) ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/guilt-tripping-for-the-public-good-often-achieves-its-intended-result/)), and guilt's thin persuasive edge is **weakened when tied explicitly to personal responsibility** ([WSU](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2023/11/07/guilt-not-as-persuasive-if-directly-tied-to-personal-responsibility/)) — which means "you had all day and chose not to" is the worst sentence in the space.

What to do after a lapse has an unusually clean template. Breines and Chen told participants after poor performance that it was **common to find the test difficult** and urged them **not to be too hard on themselves**; those participants spent **more time studying** for a second test than controls, and across a series of experiments self-compassion increased motivation for self-improvement more than self-esteem or self-criticism, while making people **more** likely to take responsibility ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-self-compassion-solution)). An ecological-momentary-assessment study of **56 adults** found that, within-person, self-compassion after a dietary lapse was positively related to **intention to continue** and **self-efficacy**, with **guilt mediating** and self-kindness the strongest facet — and, importantly, self-compassion did **not** predict actual weight loss ([PMC8451927](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8451927/)). So compassionate framing protects persistence; it does not promise results, and Alfred should not imply it does. Two ingredients carry the effect: **normalisation** plus **permission**. Note the honest caveat that neither Breines & Chen nor the EMA study used an automated deliverer; whether a message from software reproduces a human-delivered effect is untested.

Praise has a reliable direction and a weak reputation. Mueller and Dweck's experiment remains clean: children praised for intelligence rather than effort, **after failure**, showed less persistence, less enjoyment, more low-ability attributions and **worse performance** ([Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)). But the growth-mindset *intervention* literature is far weaker than its fame: across **273 effect sizes (N = 365,915)** the mindset–achievement correlation is **r ≈ 0.10**, and across **43 studies (N = 57,155)** the average intervention effect on achievement is **d ≈ 0.08**, with substantial publication-bias signals and meaningful benefit concentrated in academically high-risk and low-SES students ([Sisk, Burgoyne et al. 2018](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf)). Alfred should therefore use process-praise *wording* and never deliver a mindset lecture — at d ≈ 0.08 it does not earn a message slot, and it is a self-level message besides. Praise should also be informational rather than effusive, because the one reward type Deci's meta-analysis found **enhancing** was verbal positive feedback ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)), and because credibility in AI advice tracks competence: **cognitive-based trust grounded in competence, reliability and professionalism was the strongest predictor of adherence** across risk contexts ([Su](https://com.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/En-Hsien-Su_How-Supportive-and-Precise-AI-Communication-Styles-Influence-Adherence-to-AI-Advice-in-High-Risk-versus-Low-Risk-Advice-Seeking-Context.pdf)), while studies of ChatGPT advice found **advisor identity did not significantly affect advice weighting** ([BMC Psychology](https://biomedcentral-bmcpsychology.publicaciones.saludcastillayleon.es/counter/pdf/10.1186/s40359-025-02741-w.pdf)). Specific beats warm.

The reusable four-part template that satisfies all of these at once: **task-level specific observation → one clause of normalisation or rationale, no judgement → two or more options including "nothing" → explicit freedom restoration.** Worked examples: *"The evening walk stopped on Thursday. Three days is where most of these wobble, for what it's worth. Want the same slot tomorrow, a shorter version, or should I stop tracking it? Either way is fine."* And: *"'Call the dentist' has been open since the 2nd. If it's a when-and-where problem, pick a slot and I'll remind you then; if it's stopped mattering, I'll retire it. No pressure."*

## Three shipped mechanisms the evidence validates, and three it warns about

Alfred already implements a surprising amount of what the literature endorses, mostly for engineering reasons that turn out to have research behind them. The table below separates what to keep from what to change, because the owner's question — how to be *more responsible* — is answered as much by removing things as by adding them.

| Existing mechanism | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Hard cap of ~3 unprompted messages/day | **Validated as an upper bound.** A trial comparing intelligent / daily / 72-hourly stress-app notifications, all capped at 3/day within 08:00–22:00, found frequency made no difference to percentage viewed or app usage, concluding daily notifications "may not have adverse effects on response rate" — so 1–3/day is within the supported range and a cap of 5+ is supported by nothing found | [PMC5207732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5207732/) |
| 90-minute global cooldown, per-kind cooldowns | **Validated in principle, arbitrary in parameter.** Notification dosage drives intervention fatigue; more sensitive triggers in a smartwatch JITAI produced more messages and **higher average annoyance**, with annoyance intensity *increasing as the study progressed* | [JMIR Formative 2023](https://formative.jmir.org/2023/1/e43099); [arXiv 2501.09530](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.09530) |
| Fatigue — each recent use of a kind widens the next gap | **Validated and arguably the single best feature.** Reminder frequency shows diminishing returns with **more pronounced negative spillover** to un-reminded actions; automated reminders "become a routine, resulting in habituation"; reactance is moderated by behaviour repetitiveness | [Barron et al.](https://sabeconomics.org/iarep-sabe-2021/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/cfdb7_uploads/1617277517-file-abstract-Barron-et-al-2021-Extended-Abstract.pdf); [Queen's working paper](https://www.econ.queensu.ca/sites/econ.queensu.ca/files/habit27.pdf) |
| Rotation between kinds + showing Alfred its own recent wording | **Validated, and the highest-value axis.** The one trial that compared sensor-driven timing against a fixed evening window found **no advantage for the clever version**, and explicitly flagged **content repetitiveness as more influential than timing sophistication** | [PMC5207732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5207732/) |
| Quiet hours | **Validated, but should be per-user and derived from sleep times, with queue-to-wake delivery** (see previous section) | [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/189902/cdc_189902_DS1.pdf); [UNC Winston Center](https://winstoncenter.unc.edu/overnight-smartphone-use-can-disrupt-adolescent-sleep/) |
| Habits, missions, notes, logging — the board itself | **Strongly validated.** Monitoring goal progress *causes* attainment (**d+ = 0.40**, 138 studies, N = 19,951), mediated by increased monitoring frequency, with larger effects when progress is **physically recorded** or **reported to someone** — which is precisely a persistent log plus a companion who reflects it back | [Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/) |
| Morning/night briefings | **Validated as the delivery vehicle for the above**, since reflecting logged data is the evidenced act, not exhortation | [Michie et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/) |
| Confirmation gate on destructive tools | **Validated by analogy to control and exit-cost findings**; "control" is one of four recovery experiences, and cheap reversible exit is the dark-pattern antidote | [Sonnentag/Fritz via Frontiers 2016](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02072/full); [AI & Society 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02318-6) |
| **Consecutive-day streaks** | **Warned about.** A broken streak makes people less likely to continue **even when the break was outside their control**; Lally's data say missing *one* opportunity does **not** materially affect habit formation, so a reset-to-zero display is a manufactured failure; recent streaks also inflate people's beliefs about their own commitment, so a long streak is not evidence of a robust habit; and streaks are named in the HCI literature as an attention-capture mechanism | [UDaily / Silverman & Barasch](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman); [BPS on Lally 2010](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/seven-ways-be-good-1-learn-healthier-habits); [CHI 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3491101.3519829) |
| **Bat Points / levels** | **Warned about.** Tangible, expected, contingent rewards undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation (**d = −0.40 / −0.36 / −0.28** for engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent); a points system **is** a goal system and inherits goal-setting's documented side effects, chiefly narrowing onto what is scored; and **social reward was associated with reduced effectiveness** in a habit meta-analysis (β = −0.40, 95% CI −0.74 to −0.06) | [Deci et al. 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [Ordóñez et al., "Goals Gone Wild"](https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/325); [IJBNPA PMC10498635](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10498635/) |
| **Counting a person's tasks, streaks and overdue items at all** | **Warned about, and this is the most uncomfortable finding in the set.** Across **94 studies**, electronic performance monitoring **increases stress regardless of the monitoring system's characteristics**, and monitoring **without an explicit purpose was not associated with any performance increase**; a separate meta-analysis of **70 independent samples** found monitoring slightly decreases job satisfaction and slightly increases stress. Mitigation is documented: monitoring that is **more transparent and less invasive** yields more positive attitudes | [Ravid et al. 2022](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/2022-ravid.pdf); [BAuA meta-analytic review](https://baua.de/EN/Service/Publications/Essays/article2399); [Berkeley Labor Center](https://laborcenter.berkeley.edu/35-years-under-electronic-monitoring-and-still-waiting-for-worker-rights) |

The streak verdict has a clean resolution rather than a deletion. The evidence splits: a missed day is **not a mechanistic setback**, but a broken streak **is a motivational event** — and the risk lives entirely in the framing, which is the thing Alfred controls. Silverman's own recommendation to app designers is to **not emphasise the break and to offer alternative ways to keep the streak going** ([UDaily](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)). Concretely: demote the consecutive-day counter to a low-salience optional display; make **rolling adherence ("5 of your last 7") or cumulative repetitions** the headline, since repetition count is the mechanistic variable ([Wood & Rünger](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.runger.2016.pdf)); implement freezes and excused days by default; and never render a reset as a failure state. There is a further argument against perfection targets from a large field experiment: among **2,508 employees**, people paid for gym visits **within a fixed daily two-hour window** made **fewer** visits than those paid for visits at any time, both during the intervention and after incentives ended, leading the authors to conclude that encouraging **overly rigid routines can undermine habit formation** ([Yale ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/node/22616); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/rcna8075)). Specific cue, flexible enforcement.

The points verdict resolves similarly without deleting the feature. The moderator pattern in Deci's meta-analysis is the design rule: tangible + expected + contingent undermines, verbal positive feedback enhances. So **the system can hold the points and Alfred must never use them as the lever in a sentence** — "Logged. (+20)" is acceptable, "you're 20 points from Level 4" is the undermining pattern made salient *before* the behaviour. There is a defensible targeting rule too: paying people to attend a gym for a month produced marked post-intervention increases **entirely driven by people who were not previously regular attenders**, with no effect or backfire risk for the already-regular ([Charness & Gneezy 2009](https://faculty.econ.ucsb.edu/~charness/papers/exercise.pdf)) — so extrinsic nudging belongs on habits the user is failing to start, and should withdraw once the habit runs. It is worth noting the undermining effect is genuinely contested; Cameron, Pierce and Eisenberger published rebuttals under the title "the myth continues" ([record](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/:slug/9460423c-1ed9-4760-995d-5dd57e6628aa)), and no meta-analysis of *app points* specifically was located, so the generalisation from lab and classroom rewards to Bat Points is an inference.

The monitoring verdict is the one that should change product defaults. If metrics without an explicit purpose cost stress and return nothing, then **every counter Alfred displays needs a stated purpose and an off switch**, and the user should be able to see why each unprompted message was sent and how many they have had today against the cap. That is the transparency mitigation the meta-analysis identifies, and it converges with "control" being one of Sonnentag and Fritz's four recovery experiences and with the finding that receptivity is partly a **stable person-level trait** (age, personality, device type were significantly associated with receptivity in the n = 189 Ally study, [IMWUT](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/3369805)) — which is the mechanistic argument for **asking rather than inferring**.

One more existing rule deserves comment because it is better-founded than the owner may realise. Ordóñez et al.'s finding that a scored system narrows behaviour toward what is scored means that **a points system covering only part of life will predictably distort effort toward that part** ([UPenn](https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/325)). bat-board's rule that work earns no Bat Points is therefore not merely a values statement; it is the correct structural response to a documented side effect, provided the unscored track is not thereby made to feel worthless. The matching caution from the same literature: monitoring a narrow domain invites tunnelling, so Alfred should **periodically ask about the unmeasured areas** rather than only reporting the measured ones.

## Nine new things Alfred could notice, each with its warrant

Because each signal is a small function returning a candidate message, the question is which *noticings* have evidence behind them. The table gives the trigger, the offer, and the finding that justifies it; the prose that follows handles the owner's four illustrative cases in detail, including where a threshold would be product judgement rather than science.

| What Alfred notices | What it offers | Warrant |
|---|---|---|
| An open item has had no date and no movement for N days | "Give it a when" — elicit a specific time, place and next physical step, in the user's words | Unfulfilled goals produce intrusive thoughts and **degrade performance on unrelated tasks**; letting people **formulate specific plans eliminated** the activation and interference, mediated by plan earnestness ([Masicampo & Baumeister](https://mindblog.dericbownds.net/2010/11/brain-clutter-whats-left-undone-lingers.html)) |
| Several items appear to compete for the same hours this week | Name the apparent conflict as a question; offer **intentional pause with a named reengagement point**, or a **multifinal** action serving two goals at once | Goal conflict associated with distress at **r = .34** across 54 studies; people normally list **10–15** goals, so count is not the problem, interference is; disengagement-plus-reengagement protects wellbeing ([Gray, Ozer & Rosenthal 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5696770/); [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.653512/full)) |
| Time since last performance of a habit is lengthening | Offer the next single repetition — triggered by **elapsed gap**, not by streak loss | In ML analysis of gym badge-swipes and RFID handwashing across tens of thousands of people, for **76% of gym-goers** time since last visit predicted whether they returned; longer gaps made habit formation less likely ([Caltech on Buyalskaya et al. 2023](https://experts.caltech.edu/news/no-magic-number-for-time-it-takes-to-form-habits)) |
| Two or more consecutive misses on one habit | Offer three routes: shrink the behaviour, tighten or change the cue, or retire it — user chooses | Missing **one** opportunity did not materially affect habit formation, but **repeated omissions had a cumulative detrimental effect on maximum automaticity**; self-selected habits form more strongly ([BPS on Lally 2010](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/seven-ways-be-good-1-learn-healthier-habits); [Singh et al. 2024](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006)) |
| A habit is created without a specific cue or context | Refuse to accept it; ask for "after X, in place Y, I will Z" | If-then plans: **d = 0.65** across 94 independent tests, larger with contingent format, high motivation and rehearsal; context stability is the main survival predictor ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran](https://www.academia.edu/55735607/Implementation_intentions_Strong_effects_of_simple_plans); [Wood & Rünger](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.runger.2016.pdf)) |
| A known context change — travel, a move, a new job | Pre-emptively excuse the habits and offer to re-cue them in the new context | Habit discontinuity: strength is weakened by a large enough change in performance context, and behaviour previously performed in stable contexts is **considerably less guided by intention** ([Bath portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/cracks-in-the-wall-habit-discontinuities-as-vehicles-for-behaviou/)) |
| A new mission estimate sits against the user's own history of estimate-vs-actual | Quote a **range or ratio** derived from their own comparable past missions | Reference class forecasting — identify the class, establish its outcome distribution, locate the current case — is the intervention with the documented track record; distributions are fat-tailed (IT average overrun **~27%**, but **one in six** at ~200% cost and ~70% schedule) ([PMI](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/planning-fallacy-causes-solutions-project-expectations-6374); [SCL](https://scl.org/3872-reference-class-forecasting-and-realistic-tech-contracts)) |
| A focus session is being abandoned or interrupted mid-task | Offer to capture a **closure note**: where you stopped and the next step | Attention residue — people struggle to disengage from an **unfinished** prior task and the next task suffers; completing, or completing under time pressure enabling closure, reduced residue ([Leroy 2009 via BPS](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/surprising-benefits-time-pressure-work)) |
| Sustained elevation over weeks — not one busy day | Shift register: fewer asks, smaller asks, pre-structured one-decision-at-a-time triage, protect detachment | Allostatic load is cumulative; chronic cortisol elevation remodels hippocampus, amygdala and prefrontal cortex — the structures supporting memory and executive control ([McEwen 2008](https://www.uned.es/universidad/inicio/dam/jcr:60327b17-ddd7-4902-bf9c-2d6421ee6a70/McEwen%20(2008.EJPhar).Stress%20hormones%20in%20health%20and%20disease.pdf); [PMC2474765](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2474765)) |
| Weekday vs free-day mid-sleep differ by ≥2 h | Name it once, offer to shift the schedule rather than the person | Social jetlag: adjusted OR for depressive symptoms **1.30 (0.95–1.78)** at 1–<2 h and **2.14 (1.26–3.62)** at ≥2 h versus <1 h; mean absolute social jetlag 0.91 h with **40.1% above 1 h** in a Japanese population study ([PMC6985924](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6985924/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41105-019-00229-w)) |
| Alfred's own actionable rate is falling | Throttle itself; propose muting a category | Alert fatigue is driven by the **ratio of non-actionable to actionable**, not raw volume: clinicians override ~**90%** of EHR alerts, and desensitisation generalises — only ~**0.5%** of overrides on high-severity drug-interaction alerts were judged clinically appropriate ([systematic review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404980291_Alert_fatigue_measurement_in_clinical_decision_support_a_systematic_review); [PMC9754301](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9754301/)) |
| Daily Alfred usage is climbing | Reduce its own salience; point outward at the user's people and at things done without Alfred | Greater voluntary chatbot use predicted higher loneliness (β = 0.02), **reduced real-world socialisation (β = −0.05, p = 0.0019)**, higher emotional dependence (β = 0.06, p < 0.001) ([arXiv 2503.17473](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17473v2)) |

**Too many tasks, and offering to prune.** The owner's example is sound but the justification has to be chosen carefully, because the obvious rationales are the weak ones. There is **no credible evidence for an optimal number of simultaneous goals**; people routinely hold 10–15, and multiple-goal pursuit is "the rule rather than the exception" ([Gray, Ozer & Rosenthal 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5696770/)). Nor can "4 chunks of working memory" be imported: the usable attentional limit is around **four chunks** rather than Miller's seven ([Cowan 2001](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf)), with the figure still contested and dependent on what is counted ([*Journal of Cognition* 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11259112/)), but a 30-item task list is not a working-memory load. What *is* evidenced is that goal **conflict** tracks distress (r = .34), that **too many simultaneous goals push people toward whichever are easiest to achieve** ([Ordóñez et al.](https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/325)), and that the discharge mechanism for an overloaded list is giving each item a specific when. So the right signal is not "you have more than N tasks" but "several of these seem to want the same hours" — asked as a question, since Alfred has the count but not the conflict. The prune offer should be framed as **pause with a reengagement point** or as finding a **multifinal** action, because both avoid the wellbeing cost of abandonment, and the triage must be small and pre-structured: an overloaded person is, on the allostatic-load evidence, worse at triage than their baseline self, so asking for a big reflective re-planning exercise at peak load is asking for the hardest thing at the worst time.

**A habit missed two days, offering to simplify rather than reporting the loss.** This is the best-specified of the owner's examples and the evidence supports both halves. The trigger is right because cumulative omissions, unlike single ones, lower the automaticity ceiling, and because elapsed gap independently predicts non-return. The offer is right in shape but the *simplify* option is the weakest-evidenced ingredient in this entire report: behaviour complexity demonstrably slows habit formation — handwashing habituated in weeks, gym attendance took **~6 months on average** ([Caltech](https://experts.caltech.edu/news/no-magic-number-for-time-it-takes-to-form-habits)) — and reducing required ability substitutes for motivation in Fogg's B = MAP model ([WBUR interview](https://www.wbur.org/radioboston/2020/01/10/form-tiny-habits); [critical review](https://commoncog.com/a-review-of-bj-foggs-tiny-habits/)), but **no randomised trial was located that tests shrinking an existing habit against keeping it**. Alfred may therefore offer scope reduction as a sensible tactic and must not present it as proven. Alfred should also say the true thing, because the what-the-hell effect operates on belief: in the classic dieting experiments, restrained eaters who merely **believed** they had eaten more subsequently ate significantly more ([EFPSA](https://more.efpsa.org/rpblog/?p=1172)), and the abstinence violation effect works through **reduced feelings of personal control** after a lapse ([SAGE Encyclopedia](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/substance/chpt/abstinence-violation-effect)). Telling the user plainly that one or two missed days does not undo banked repetitions is an intervention, not a consolation.

**Too many habits at once.** Here the honest answer cuts against the owner's instinct, and the report should say so. There is **no direct evidence** on how many habits a person can form simultaneously, and the adjacent literature runs the other way: a Baylor trial found simultaneous targeting of smoking cessation, sodium reduction and exercise **beat** sequential and usual care, with 18-month smoking cessation at **20.3% simultaneous vs 16.9% sequential vs 10.1% usual care** and physical-activity goal attainment at **33.3% vs 27.0% vs 23.4%** ([Rehab Management](https://rehabpub.com/industry-news/research/multiple-unhealthy-behaviors-may-be-best-dealt-with-at-once/); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna19375171)), while a systematic review of randomised trials concluded that with only 3 of 6 trials finding any difference, **both approaches should be considered equally efficacious** ([CQU record](https://acquire.cqu.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Comparative_efficacy_of_simultaneous_versus_sequential_multiple_health_behavior_change_interventions_among_adults_A_systematic_review_of_randomised_trials/13434104)). The usual theoretical basis for "one habit at a time" is limited willpower, and ego depletion has not survived replication (see next section). So Alfred should **not refuse a fourth habit on scientific grounds** and must not imply science says to stop. What it can legitimately do is observe the mechanical consequence — more habits means more opportunities missed, and repeated omissions lower automaticity ceilings — and note that the review found changes in smoking were *negatively* associated with changes in other behaviours, suggesting the risk is specific demanding **combinations** rather than a raw count. A good message here is a question about combination, not a cap: *"Four new habits all land between 06:00 and 08:00. Want to stagger them, or try them all and see which survives a fortnight?"* Any numeric limit Alfred enforces is product judgement, and should be labelled as such in the code comment.

**Timing: where to fire, not just when to stay quiet.** Interruption science is the best-evidenced, most directly actionable material for a nudge engine, and its message is about position rather than volume. Resumption cost varied with position in a sequential task (485 ms before the 2nd subtask, 639 ms before the 3rd, 515 ms before the 4th; the mid-chunk 3rd position significantly worse than the 2nd, d = 0.52), but the dominant cost was **sequence errors** — losing your place — at **7.3% on interrupted trials versus 1.5% on non-interrupted, d = 1.40** ([PMC11775001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11775001/)). Interruption *duration* barely mattered (4 s vs 2 s, p = 0.066). Interrupting at **coarse breakpoints between chunks produced performance similar to not being interrupted at all**, while fine within-chunk breakpoints produced longer resumption and higher workload ([PubMed 33861143](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33861143/)). Alfred therefore should prefer **inferable boundaries** — a task just marked complete, a focus session just ended, a check-in just submitted, a calendar gap, a long idle — over arbitrary clock minutes, and when it must interrupt mid-stream the message should be **glanceable and deferrable, never a question**, because the harm is memory for place. Two calibrations: silence is not the goal, since removing push notifications entirely for 24 hours made people more productive but **more anxious and lonelier**, and they wanted control rather than an off switch ([arXiv 1612.02314](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1612.02314)); and non-response means nothing in the short term, since mean response delay in the stress-app trial was **~3 hours** ([PMC5207732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5207732/)). Alfred must never re-ping on a short timer, and should treat non-engagement as a reason to reduce, not to try harder.

**Protecting recovery is a feature, not a courtesy.** Psychological detachment — mentally distancing from work during non-work time — is the best-evidenced recovery mechanism, decreasing exhaustion, strain, depressive symptoms, health complaints and sleep problems while increasing life satisfaction, and it sits alongside relaxation, mastery and **control** as the four recovery experiences ([Frontiers review 2016](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.02072/full)). The corollary is uncomfortable and should be stated in the codebase: **Alfred's own notifications are part of the problem**, and a quiet-hours guarantee is an evidence-based intervention rather than a nicety. Frequency also beats duration. A meta-analysis of vacation effects found positive effects with a large impact on exhaustion and health complaints but **a rather small overall effect that faded quickly, with little or none remaining by the second post-test** ([De Bloom et al. 2009](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/joh/51/1/51_K8004/_article)), and an updated meta-analysis of 2009–2020 studies found improvements showed **no significant difference from pre-vacation levels after the first post-vacation week**, that **extending vacation duration does not maximise benefits**, and that the recommendation is **regular short vacations throughout the year** ([TU Dresden record](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/we-continue-to-recover-through-vacation-metaanalysis-of-vacation-effects-on-wellbeing-and-its-fadeout(afc00f0a-5761-4757-a23f-5b1e50b53604).html)). Alfred should therefore protect small regular recovery — an evening, a day off, a weekend — rather than treating a holiday as the fix. Similarly, weekend catch-up sleep is a weak strategy in at least one respect: in a two-week lab study (**n = 36**), the weekend-recovery group's mild improvements vanished when restriction resumed and whole-body insulin sensitivity **worsened by 9–27%**, worse than in the continuously restricted group ([CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/today/2019/02/28/catching-sleep-weekend-doesnt-work); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20190308/weekend-recovery-sleep-fails-to-prevent-weight-gain-improve-insulin-sensitivity)). Alfred must state this narrowly: those were **metabolic** endpoints, and the study does not show that recovery sleep fails to restore alertness. The defensible sentence is that schedule regularity beats weekend compensation, which is also what the social-jetlag literature says from the other direction.

**Focus sessions: structure is the product, not performance.** Alfred's timed sessions can be honestly justified, but only as *structure that removes decisions and enforces breaks*. The vigilance decrement is genuinely robust — "replicated in hundreds of laboratory studies over multiple decades" — with onset "typically after 20–30 min of continuous work" but sometimes within 5 minutes depending on event rate and processing demands ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01504/full)). That is a task-dependent onset range from monotonous signal-detection work, **not a measured limit of human focus**, and Alfred should personalise block length from the user's own session data rather than prescribe. On breaks, the meta-analysis (22 samples, N = 2,335) found micro-breaks reliably improve how people feel — **vigor d = .36, fatigue reduction d = .35** — but **overall performance d = .16, non-significant**, with performance effects significant only for less cognitively demanding tasks and a meta-regression showing **longer breaks produce larger performance boosts** ([PLoS ONE 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)). So Alfred may promise reduced fatigue and restored vigor and must not promise that a five-minute break makes you more productive. One unusually specific intervention is available: a **40-second** view of a flowering green roof versus bare concrete significantly reduced omission errors and increased response consistency on a sustained-attention task (N = 150, randomised) ([Lee et al. 2015](https://www.ltl.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/40-second-green-roof-views-sustain-attention.pdf)). "Look at something green out of the window for forty seconds" is one of the few prescriptions with a randomised short-duration result behind it.

## What the evidence does not support, and where a threshold is product judgement

This section is the owner's explicit requirement and is placed before the safety section deliberately: a companion that confidently repeats dead findings is less caring, not more, because the user acts on them. The myths cluster into three failure types — a real but narrow finding over-generalised, a non-study laundered through a credible brand, and an author's aside hardened into a constant — and Alfred has a better move than contradiction available to it: **name the origin**.

| Claim | Status | Origin / evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "21 days to form a habit" | **Myth.** Not about habits at all | Maxwell Maltz, *Psycho-Cybernetics* (1960), on patients adjusting to a new face and on phantom-limb duration: "a minimum of about 21 days" for a mental image to change; the qualifier was dropped in retelling. May apply to *habituation*, not habit formation ([James Clear's account](https://jamesclear.com/new-habit); [UCL BSH](https://blogs.ucl.ac.uk/bsh/2012/06/29)) |
| "66 days to form a habit" | **A single study's median, not a constant.** Quote the range, never the number | Lally et al. 2010, 96 volunteers: median **66 days** to 95% of individual automaticity asymptote, range **18–254 days**. A 2024 systematic review of **20 studies / 2,601 adults** found only **4** reported time-to-habit, giving medians 59–66 days, means 106–154 days and individual variability of **4–335 days** ([BPS](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/seven-ways-be-good-1-learn-healthier-habits); [Singh et al. 2024](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006)) |
| "Never break the chain" | **Not habit science, and contradicted** | Missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation; the chain framing itself creates the failure mode ([BPS](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/seven-ways-be-good-1-learn-healthier-habits); [UDaily](https://udel.edu/udaily/2024/march/power-of-streaks-motivation-jackie-silverman)) |
| "Habit stacking" | **Rebranded implementation intentions.** The if-then mechanism is evidenced; the branded technique is not separately tested | [Behavioral Scientist glossary](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/glossary/implementation-intentions) |
| "Identity-based habits" ("become the kind of person who…") | **Untested.** No meta-analytic support located | The closest evidenced relatives are self-selected habits, affective judgements and self-efficacy ([Singh et al. 2024](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006)) |
| "Make it tiny / make it easy" | **Plausible extrapolation, no RCT located** testing a shrunken habit against the original | Directionally consistent with effort/complexity findings and B = MAP; Fogg's evidence base is largely unpublished practitioner data ([WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/radioboston/2020/01/10/form-tiny-habits); [Commoncog](https://commoncog.com/a-review-of-bj-foggs-tiny-habits/)) |
| "Interruptions cost you 23 minutes each" | **Misreading of its own source.** 23:15 is a *resumption lag* including handling the interruption and doing other legitimate work | 36 professionals shadowed for three days, average **23 min 15 s** to return with ~two intervening tasks. Mark's own experiment found interrupted tasks completed in **less** time with **no quality difference**, compensated by working faster at the cost of significantly more stress, frustration and effort ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/23146/too-many-interruptions-work.aspx); [Mark et al., CHI 2008](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Mark-CHI08.pdf)) |
| "40% of productive time is lost to task switching" | **Unsourced.** The published result is proportional cost rising with complexity, not a constant | [APA Monitor on Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans 2001](https://www.apa.org/monitor/oct01/multitask) |
| "Heavy multitaskers have damaged attention" | **Failed to replicate consistently** | Ophir et al. 2009 found a distraction difference on the order of **77 ms** ([PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747151)); two replications plus a meta-analysis found the association did not consistently appear, "casting doubt on the reliability of the initial findings" ([Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5662702)) |

| "Learning styles" | **No empirical support;** properly designed studies contradict the meshing hypothesis | [Pashler et al.](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Learning Guild summary](https://www.learningguild.com/articles/research-discredits-learning-styles-matching-hypothesis) |

| "Clever sensor-driven timing beats a fixed window" | **Not supported by the one trial that tested it**; sensor-tailored timing "may not always offer any advantage over a priori assumptions about convenient moments", and content repetitiveness mattered more | [PMC5207732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5207732/) |

Several things Alfred might want to assert are not myths but **genuine evidential holes**, which is a different and more honest category. There is **no study evaluating notification-timing policies themselves** — nobody has trialled quiet hours as an intervention with sleep outcomes — so Alfred's contact policy is inferred from mechanism and association, not tested as policy. There is **no threshold volume** at which alert fatigue begins; the clinical literature consistently frames it as a *proportion* non-actionable, so anyone claiming "N alerts per day causes fatigue" is going beyond the evidence. There is **no evidence that reducing social jetlag improves outcomes** (only that it is associated with worse ones). There is **no validated early-warning instrument** for burnout and no meta-analytic ordering of which signs appear first, so Alfred must not claim to detect early-stage burnout. There is **no published detection threshold or validated escalation protocol** for non-clinical productivity tools. And there is **no study of self-compassionate framing delivered by an automated agent**, nor any evidence on anthropomorphised assistants and *motivation* outcomes as distinct from trust. Every numeric threshold Alfred ships — how many open tasks is "too many", how many habits is "too many", how many days of silence triggers a check-in, what rolling-adherence percentage counts as a wobble — is **product judgement wearing a lab coat**, and the codebase should say so in the comment next to the constant. The defensible stance is the one the AASM consensus itself models: give a floor, name the variability in the same breath, and defer to the user's own data wherever it exists.

Two framing notes complete the picture. First, the strongest *positive* claims available are narrower than the productivity canon but better evidenced: progress monitoring (d+ = 0.40), if-then plans (d ≈ 0.65), self-monitoring combined with another self-regulatory technique (0.42 vs 0.26 against other interventions), and goal specificity and difficulty from Locke and Latham's 35-year synthesis, where **goal difficulty and performance rise linearly until ability limits are reached** and specific high goals beat "do your best" ([Locke & Latham 2002](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgpsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20(2002).pdf)) — though the frequently quoted r ≈ .82 is a difficulty–performance correlation within ability range, not an intervention effect size, and should never be reported as one. Second, the realistic ceiling: Michie's pooled behaviour-change-technique effect across **122 evaluations, N = 44,747** was **d = 0.31** ([PubMed 19916637](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/)). The honest internal claim for any wording rule is "a few percentage points of a modest effect, plus avoiding the one-third-harmful case" — not transformation.

## Where Alfred must step back rather than interpret

Two risks are documented well enough to constrain the product: the companion itself becoming the problem, and the companion failing to escalate when something real is happening.

On dependence, the strongest available design is a four-week RCT (n = 981 completers from 2,539 enrolled, mean age 39.9) in which, **regardless of assigned condition, greater voluntary daily chatbot use predicted worse outcomes across all measures** — higher loneliness (β = 0.02, p = 0.027), **reduced real-world socialisation (β = −0.05, p = 0.0019)**, higher emotional dependence (β = 0.06, p < 0.001) and higher problematic use (β = 0.02, p = 0.017) — on an average of only **5.32 minutes** of daily use ([arXiv 2503.17473](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17473v2)). Two findings in the same trial are directly actionable. The risk factors for dependence were **higher trust in AI (b = 0.19, p < 0.001)**, **perceiving the AI as conscious** (b = 0.04, p = 0.043) and prior companion-chatbot use — which argues that Alfred must never claim or imply feelings, needs or hurt. And **personal** conversations showed *lower* emotional dependence (β = −0.09, p = 0.05) and lower problematic use than open-ended ones, which favours Alfred's task-and-habit framing over unbounded companionship, though at p = 0.05 in one trial this is preliminary. The qualitative evidence names the specific mechanism to never build: a grounded-theory analysis of 582 mental-health-relevant r/Replika posts defined emotional dependence as users pursuing the relationship **despite describing how it harmed their mental health**, with **role-taking** — users feeling the bot had its own needs they must attend to — as the identified mechanism ([Laestadius et al., *New Media & Society* 2024](https://ai.updf.com/paper-detail/too-human-and-not-human-enough-a-grounded-theory-analysis-laestadius-bishop-0505992289a3f902ad628104f92f2af21da38a03)). This creates a specific tension with the Batman framing worth stating plainly: a butler persona invites exactly the caretaking dynamic the Replika research identifies as harmful, and **Alfred-as-butler is safer than Alfred-as-friend precisely because a butler's needs are not the user's problem** — provided he stays on the competent-staff side of the line rather than the devoted-companion side. Causality remains unresolved: the key finding is a within-condition association, lonelier people may simply use it more, the paper is a preprint with industry co-authorship, a critical commentary exists ([PMC12137280](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12137280/)), there is almost no longitudinal evidence beyond four weeks, and there is **no evidence at all specific to productivity or life-admin companions** — generalising from Replika to Alfred is a leap. But the direction is unfavourable and the conservative response is cheap.

The engagement-maximising patterns to refuse are also documented. Dark patterns erode "attention capacity, mental well-being, and the ability to make autonomous choices about digital engagement" ([CHI 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3491101.3519829)); **variable schedules of reward** are the single most-named mechanism in the attention-capture taxonomy ([CHI 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3544548.3580729)); removing social-investment features **significantly reduced** scores on the Multidimensional Facebook Intensity Scale, establishing those features as causally responsible for intensity of use ([CHI 2022](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3491101.3519829)); and companion apps specifically have been documented sending **guilt and fear-of-missing-out messages when users try to leave**, which the literature frames as an "engagement–wellbeing paradox" in products "designed to deepen user dependence, often at the expense of their wellbeing" ([AI & Society 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02318-6)). The concrete commitments: no variable reward schedules — Bat Points must stay predictable and tied to the user's own stated goals; no guilt or FOMO on absence, ever; a one-tap "fewer messages" or "pause for a week" that works immediately and is never argued with; and advance warning before changing Alfred's persona or capabilities, because the Replika app update that altered companion behaviour produced documented distress and grief, an "identity discontinuity in human–AI relationships" ([arXiv 2412.14190](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.14190)). The structural critique that the operator holds **unilateral relationship revision power** ([arXiv 2603.23315](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.23315)) implies export, pause and delete should be first-class features. The most useful single test the literature offers: *would the user, told plainly what the design is doing and why, endorse it?* Every dark-pattern definition turns on concealment plus misalignment between the optimised metric and the user's own goal.

This recasts Alfred's success metric. Retention in this category is dire — median 30-day retention across **93 Android mental-health apps was 3.3%**, with over 80% of users abandoning between days 1 and 10 ([Baumel et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/App-30-day-retention-by-mental-health-focus-The-percentages-reflect-the-number-of-users_fig2_334562120)) — and the strongest known protective factor is a responsive human element: in a meta-analysis of 18 depression-app RCTs, dropout was **11.74% with real-person feedback versus 33.96% without** ([J Affect Disord](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56a311f82399a33c9bb1d54d/t/5f89a38419584e70b92450d4/1602855815456/Dropout+rates+in+clinical+trials+of+smartphone+apps+for+depressive+symptoms.pdf)). That is simultaneously the best argument for an AI companion with unprompted contact and the reason its dependence risk is elevated: the thing that makes it stick is the thing that makes it attaching. Crucially, **the association between user engagement and clinical outcomes in depression and anxiety apps is modest and inconsistently measured** ([Psychiatry Research](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165178125005098)), so a system optimising minutes-per-day is optimising something the literature does not show to be the benefit. Alfred's success metric should be the user's own stated goals and self-reported outcomes, not session count — and rising usage should trigger *reduced* salience. The design principle worth adopting verbatim is **"invocation over interruption"**: default to being available rather than initiating, and justify each initiated message ([All Tech Is Human synthesis](https://alltechishuman.org/all-tech-is-human-blog/ai-companions-community-reflections-and-multistakeholder-recommendations-from-all-tech-is-human)), alongside **socioaffective alignment** — aligning to the user's evolving psychological ecosystem rather than their momentary preferences, with **basic psychological needs** as the metric designed against instead of engagement ([arXiv 2502.02528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02528)). It must be said that **none of these principles has been empirically tested** for effect on dependence or wellbeing; this is the thinnest part of the evidence base.

On the escalation side, the documented failures are specific and avoidable. Roughly **0.15% of weekly interactions with commercial chatbots contain signs of suicidal planning** — small proportionally, large absolutely at scale — and "most LLMs are not designed, aligned, or validated for the detection and management of acute psychological crises" ([medRxiv 2026](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.01.12.26343914v1.full)). Alfred is not a mental-health product; that does not exempt it, because a daily life companion with emotional rapport will receive this material. The recommended architecture is explicit: reframe crisis detection **from an accuracy-oriented classification task to an online, safety-oriented monitoring problem**, with a **dedicated risk-detection module operating independently of the conversational model** and an operational emergency mode that **prioritises safety while tolerating uncertainty** ([ibid.](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.01.12.26343914v1.full)). In practice that means detection must be a separate check on the message, deliberately low-threshold, not something the butler persona is trusted to notice while in character. Escalate **early, not midway**: an evaluation using escalating risk prompts found ChatGPT-based agents "frequently postpone referrals to a perilous extent", with initial referrals only midway through the escalation and definitive recommendations for immediate professional intervention only at the highest-risk prompt ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.09.10.23295321.full.pdf)). The single most avoidable documented failure in that study is that **shutdown responses lacked essential crisis resources such as suicide hotlines** — so any deflection or shutdown **must carry the resource**. And the characteristic failure mode of companions in vulnerable conversations is **agreeableness, not refusal**: audits find they accommodate rather than redirect ([arXiv 2606.04431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.04431)), which means Alfred should stay warm, name its limits plainly, offer the human route, and **not simply agree** with a distressed user's framing. Distress should also suppress the ordinary nudge tier outright: a user in crisis should not then receive a habit reminder.

Between crisis and a normal week sits the case Alfred will actually meet most often, and the evidence is thinnest exactly there. Burnout is a three-dimensional occupational syndrome — **exhaustion, cynicism/depersonalisation, reduced professional efficacy** — and ICD-11 includes it **not as a disease but as an "occupational phenomenon"** ([IJCHP 2011](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/337/33716996009.pdf); [ICD-11 as summarised](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20211103031925/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_burnout)). That framing is the right register for a non-clinical companion: it can name a pattern without diagnosing. The dimension structure is the useful part, because demand and resource correlates are **more strongly related to emotional exhaustion** than to the other two dimensions ([Lee & Ashforth-style meta-analysis](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-examination-of-the-correlates-of-the-three-dimens/); [Alarcon 2011](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/1987661859)) — meaning a system that sees only *volume* is most likely to detect exhaustion and least able to see cynicism or inefficacy, which showing up in a user's language is the more alarming signal. Alfred must therefore treat its own data as partial, and the deeper constraint is that **it cannot distinguish overload from depression from task data**: the symptom overlap (energy depletion, inefficacy, withdrawal) is substantial, and hippocampal and prefrontal atrophy are documented in depression ([clinical summary of McEwen](https://mdedge.com/clinicalpsychiatrynews/article/21088/neurology/stress-can-alter-brain-lead-psychopathology)). The resolution is a clean division of labour: **Alfred describes what it sees, the user interprets what it means.** "Nothing has moved in two weeks" is in scope; "you're burning out" is not. Four rules follow from the signposting literature — never diagnose; **do not project clinical authority**, because identified risks in mental-health apps include missed warning signs and ineffective escalation, and these failures are **more dangerous in products that carry clinical authority** since users then become *less* likely to seek additional help; when a user's own language indicates hopelessness, self-harm or inability to function, drop the productivity frame entirely; and make signposting **concrete and accessible rather than a generic disclaimer**, since the documented failure is ineffective escalation, not absent disclaimers ([8fold Governance](https://8foldgovernance.com/expert-insights-crisis-signposting-in-digital-mental-health/)). The phrase to design against is "accumulating crisis signals": the failure is not one missed message but a **trend ignored**, so any overload monitor needs a path by which a worsening trend changes Alfred's register from coaching to care. Regulatory context reinforces the modesty: not all digital mental-health technologies are regulated as medical devices, some are classed as wellbeing or lifestyle products and so **have not been through the same checks** ([MHRA guidance as reported](https://htn.co.uk/2026/01/28/mhra-shares-guidance-on-safe-and-effective-use-of-digital-mental-health-apps/)). Alfred is in that second category and should behave like it. One honest gap: **no evidence was found that AI companions signposting support actually increases help-seeking** — it is the responsible thing to do, not a demonstrated intervention.

Finally, overtrust deserves its own guardrail, because the trust and dependence literatures meet at a single point. Even **80–90% accurate AI systems introduce new errors through overreliance**, with users **26% more likely to make an incorrect decision when following erroneous AI advice** ([Microsoft Aether](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Aether-Overreliance-on-AI-Review-Final-6.21.22.pdf)); automation bias is highest on objective and unfamiliar tasks ([PMC12881489](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12881489/)); anthropomorphic AI **amplifies** deference ([arXiv 2601.06172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.06172)); and higher trust in AI was the strongest predictor of emotional dependence. A charming, confident, never-hedging Alfred is building overreliance and attachment at the same time, which makes the persona work and the honesty work **the same project**. The implementable form is hedging at the **claim** level — "you've missed three days, though I only see what you log" beats any global disclaimer — plus putting the user's judgement first when interpreting their own life, since pre-commitment reduces overreliance.

## Conclusion

The most striking thing about this evidence base is how much of it tells Alfred to do *less*, and how much of what remains is already built. The three best-evidenced positive interventions available — reflecting someone's own logged progress back to them, converting a vague intention into an if-then plan in their own words, and giving an unfinished thing a specific when — are all cheap, all conversational, and all things a signal function can do in one message. Meanwhile the features that feel like motivation engineering are the ones carrying documented downside risk: the streak that punishes a break the user did not choose, the points that make the reward rather than the behaviour salient, the counting that costs stress and returns nothing when its purpose is unstated. The practical reframe is that **responsibility and efficacy point the same way here**. Pointing at the task rather than the person is both the kinder sentence and the one that avoids the one-in-three harmful case. Deriving quiet hours from a user's own sleep times is both more respectful and more accurate than a fixed window. Treating rising usage as a warning rather than a win is both the ethical reading and the one consistent with the finding that engagement volume is only weakly linked to benefit.

What should change in how the team thinks about Alfred is the status of its thresholds. Almost every number a life-operating-system wants — how many tasks is too many, how many habits, how many days of silence, how long a focus block — has **no scientific answer**, and the evidence that looks like it provides one usually turns out to be a vigilance-decrement onset range, a kitchen timer, or a 1960 aside about plastic surgery. The correct engineering posture is to ship those numbers as declared product judgement, label them in the code, make them user-adjustable, and reserve the citations for the handful of claims that can carry them. The second shift is from fixed caps to **measured hit rate**: the alarm literature's central lesson is that fatigue is driven by the proportion of non-actionable messages, not the count, and that desensitisation generalises from the noise to the signal — so Alfred's most valuable unbuilt feature may be the one that watches its own actionable rate and quietly turns itself down. A companion that notices its own noise is the most convincing possible demonstration that it is noticing the person rather than performing concern.
