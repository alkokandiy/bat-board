# TickTick rewards completion, penalises delay, never adapts

TickTick is an integration play, not a mechanics play: tasks, habits, a Pomodoro timer, an Eisenhower matrix and a multi-object calendar share one store, and the clever parts are nearly all about *reducing the cost of an action* rather than about modelling behaviour. Its most transferable single decision is that **habit streaks are evaluated against the frequency target the user set, not against consecutive calendar days** — meeting "3× per week" every week keeps a streak alive and a week with two check-ins breaks it ([TickTick blog, 2020-11-06](https://blog.ticktick.com/2020/11/06/stuck-to-habits-by-going-digital/)). Its second is that it offers **three separate repairs for unrecorded focus time** — extend a finished session, shrink an overrun, or create a record from nothing — and buries the only one that helps a user who never started a timer. Its clearest failure, and the one that matters most to bat-board, is that **TickTick's reminder system has no back-off whatsoever**: it collects exactly the signals needed to notice that a user is ignoring an alert (postpone counts, task age) and spends them on a panel the user has to open deliberately, while its flagship Premium reminder feature deliberately bypasses Silent Mode and Do Not Disturb and repeats "until you take action" ([TickTick Help: Constant Reminder](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7374017550633402368)). Meanwhile its own gamification history is a cautionary tale told in three guardrails: in 2017 TickTick narrowed the Achievement Score to "completed tasks and overdue tasks only" ([TickTick blog, 2017-10-18](https://blog.ticktick.com/2017/10/18/say-hi-to-ticktick-40/)), then added daily anti-farming caps to badges, then firewalled other people's actions out of your score — three measures nobody builds until a counter is being gamed. The research underneath this report is strong on documented mechanics and weak on user sentiment, for one specific reason stated plainly below: **reddit.com was unreachable to every researcher on this project**, so nothing here should be read as a survey of what TickTick users actually complain about.

## Habits judge you against your own frequency target, and offer no rest day

TickTick's habit model is narrower than its reputation. There are exactly **three recurrence modes — Daily (with day-of-week selection), Weekly (N times per week), and Interval (every N days)** — serialised as iCalendar RRULEs with one proprietary extension, `TT_TIMES=n`, carrying the weekly count; a live habit object shows `"repeatRule": "RRULE:FREQ=WEEKLY;INTERVAL=1;TT_TIMES=2"` ([dida_api habit.md](https://github.com/luke1879012/dida_api/blob/HEAD/docs/web/habit/habit.md), [ticktick-sdk habits-api.md](https://github.com/dev-mirzabicer/ticktick-sdk/blob/HEAD/docs/api-analysis/habits-api.md)). Because the weekly form carries **no `BYDAY`**, TickTick does not pre-assign which days count: any check-in consumes one of the n slots. What is conspicuously **not** offered is monthly-by-date, monthly-by-nth-weekday, yearly, or "X times per month" — a December 2024 r/ticktick thread asking for monthly habits drew only a user's suggestion to set a 15-day interval, and that thread could not be loaded first-hand, so treat its content as second-hand.

Goals come in two shapes. `type` is either `Boolean` ("done or not done") or `Real`, where the habit carries a `goal`, a per-tap `step` and a free-text `unit` — all **doubles in the API**, so 2.5 km in 0.5 steps is representable ([activepieces habit-fields.ts](https://github.com/activepieces/activepieces/blob/HEAD/packages/pieces/community/ticktick/src/lib/common/habit-fields.ts)). Three input modes sit on top of the same storage: **Automatic Recording** (each swipe adds `step`), **Manual Recording** (type the amount), and **Complete All** (one-shot) ([Better Achieve Habits](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781805944733696)). Partial progress is first-class in the *record* and worthless in the *score*: a HAR capture shows the first increment (value 0→1 of goal 5) leaving `totalCheckIns: 0` and `currentStreak: 0`, with both incrementing only when `value` reached the goal, and a partial row carrying `status: 0` with no `checkinTime` ([habits-api.md](https://github.com/dev-mirzabicer/ticktick-sdk/blob/HEAD/docs/api-analysis/habits-api.md)). There is no "at most" mode: the completion test is `value >= goal`, so a bad habit must be inverted by hand. Notably there is **no description field at all** — the only prose slot is a single short `encouragement` string captured on the first creation screen, surfaced as a reward message rather than as a "why" the user re-reads.

The day-state model is thin and the research is honest about where it stops. Storage supports no row, `status: 0` with a partial value, and `status: 2` complete; `1` was never observed, so **do not assume a skip state exists in the enum**. A habit-level `exDates` array exists and most plausibly excludes dates from the schedule, which would make an excluded day unscheduled rather than failed. A 2020 blog post documents an explicit "**Mark as Unachieved**" state recording a miss "with the time and reason" ([TickTick blog, 2020-05-22](https://blog.ticktick.com/2020/05/22/a-healthier-life-with-ticktick-habit/)), but a verbatim search of the entire current help-centre corpus found **zero occurrences of "streak", "unachieved" or "skip" in a habit context** — the only documented Skip is "Skip this Recurrence" for recurring *tasks*. Whether the 2026 app still offers Unachieved is unresolved. A "freeze button" grace day is claimed by one auto-generated SEO site and corroborated nowhere; the best current evidence is that **TickTick has no rest-day, freeze or grace mechanic**.

Streaks carry two unresolved problems. First, a direct conflict in TickTick's own description: the blog says the streak "will increase by one every time you check it off" while also defining the break condition weekly, and those two readings cannot be reconciled from the sources — for a 3×/week habit it is unclear whether the counter counts check-ins or successful weeks. Second, `currentStreak` and `totalCheckIns` are **denormalised integers written by the client**, not derived server-side: on goal completion the app itself POSTs the habit with both incremented ([habits-api.md](https://github.com/dev-mirzabicer/ticktick-sdk/blob/HEAD/docs/api-analysis/habits-api.md)). Whether a back-filled day repairs a broken streak is unverified and, given the absence of any server recompute, probably not. There is no `bestStreak` field in the captured object even though best streak is displayed, so it is computed elsewhere.

Everything else about habits is sober and worth copying wholesale. Back-fill is **unlimited in horizon** — you tap any date in the per-habit monthly calendar, and check-ins are keyed by `checkinStamp` (YYYYMMDD) with a separate `opTime` for when the write happened, so a back-dated entry is auditable; one captured record was written at `opTime 2026-01-17T13:37` carrying `checkinStamp: 20260116`. Archive and delete are cleanly distinguished and documented verbatim: delete removes the habit "along with all its check-in records" irreversibly, while archive hides it, preserves everything and is restorable from an **Archived** section ([Better Achieve Habits](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781805944733696)); internally that is `status: 2` plus `archivedTime`, and a real archived habit retained `totalCheckIns: 45`. Reminders are a bare array of `"HH:MM"` strings, multiple per habit (the docs' own example is water at 9:00, 12:00, 18:00 and 21:00) with **no snooze, no adaptivity and no auto-fail** — a missed day simply has no row. Grouping is the one design constraint worth refusing: sections are a **fixed morning/afternoon/evening/other taxonomy** (server-side literals `_morning`, `_afternoon`, `_night`), not user-named folders. Review comes in three tiers — a per-habit monthly table, a per-habit yearly heat map, and an all-habits Week/Month/Record screen whose **Month view is Premium-gated and mobile-only**, with TickTick stating flatly that Weekly and Monthly views are "Currently, only mobile app is supported" ([Habit Review](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781785312952320)). The commercial lever is blunt: **5 habits free, 299 on Premium**, confirmed by Apple's own listing of "Add more than 5 habits" as a Premium feature ([Mac App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id966085870)).

## The Eisenhower matrix is a saved filter that writes back when you drag

The matrix is not a container and tasks have no quadrant field. It is **four independent AND-filters over the normal task store**, and TickTick says so: "the Eisenhower Matrix is simply another way to view your tasks. Tasks remain in their original lists and aren't duplicated or moved" ([How to Use Eisenhower Matrix](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782055577124864)). Two rule presets ship. **Rule Combination 1 (the default) maps priority to quadrant only** — which means out of the box the matrix is a priority view wearing matrix clothing, one axis of data drawn on two. **Rule Combination 2 adds time**: "Priority determines how important a task is. Time determines how urgent a task is", and placement is then re-evaluated as dates approach, so "a task that was initially not urgent may move to an urgent quadrant" ([How to Edit the Rules](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782040439881728)). Dragging between quadrants is a **write-back**: TickTick "automatically adjusts the relevant task attributes to match the target quadrant's rules", mutating priority under rule set 1 and date plus priority under rule set 2, and drags can simply **fail** when a quadrant's rules name another assignee. Because the quadrants are independent filters rather than a partition, duplication is designed in — "If a task matches the rules of multiple quadrants, it will appear in each of them" — and a task matching none appears nowhere, a case TickTick never mentions. Up to **nine custom matrix views** shipped on 2 November, each with independent rules ([Create and Manage Eisenhower Matrix Views](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7519291357756653568)).

Two cautions belong on the record. TickTick's own pages **name the four quadrants inconsistently** ("Urgent & Important" versus "Important & Urgent" and so on) across the two articles that define them. More importantly, **the default priority-to-quadrant mapping is undocumented**: only one leg is stated anywhere ("High Priority → Urgent & Important"), and which of Medium, Low and None lands in each remaining quadrant is unverified. The four-priorities/four-quadrants symmetry makes a 1:1 mapping likely, and the ambiguous case is exactly the interesting one — whether Medium reads as important-but-not-urgent or as urgent-but-unimportant. Do not treat any circulating mapping table as confirmed.

The rest of the task model is well documented. Priorities are a single four-valued enum — **High, Medium, Low, None** ([Task Details](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782224016441344)) — and the existence of a separate **Pin** ("always display at the top"), explicitly motivated by the FAQ "Too Many High-Priority Tasks, How to Quickly Find the Most Important One?", is a design confession: a four-level enum saturates in practice, and TickTick's answer was an orthogonal boolean rather than more levels. Recurrence is where TickTick genuinely leads: Custom Repeat offers three *engines* — **By Due Dates**, **By Completion Date** ("the next reminder time calculated based on the time the previous task was completed"), and **By Specific Dates** — with completion-based repeat available for all patterns and in the product since 2013 ([Set Up Recurring Tasks](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782206349770752), [TickTick blog, 2013-10-29](https://blog.ticktick.com/2013/10/29/repeat-your-tasks-upon-completion-date/)). The published worked example is unambiguous: an every-3-days task done Tuesday falls next on Friday; completed Saturday instead, the next is Tuesday. The series is modelled as one row with a cursor, not materialised occurrences, which is why **"you cannot modify future recurring tasks until the current recurrence is completed"** and missed occurrences stack up to be ticked or skipped one at a time ([FAQ](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781708385140736)). One FAQ on that page contradicts the article's own sections by explaining "Repeat Ends On" versus "Repeat Ends After xx times" in terms of late-completion rescheduling; it reads as a mistranslation and should not be treated as the real mechanic.

Subtasks and checklist items are two different things with one sharp, surprising rule. Check items are a **mode of the body field** — a task cannot have both a rich description and check items — while subtasks are full tasks nestable five levels deep with their own dates, tags, assignees and focus sessions ([Multilevel Tasks](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782361849495552)). The roll-up is verbatim: "only the completion of the check items will affect the task progress. After all check items are completed, the parent task will be automatically completed." So five of eight subtasks done contributes **nothing** to parent progress, and if you want progress you must give up dates and assignees. Tags are many-to-many, colour-coded and capped at exactly **two levels** ("second-level tags"), and deleting a tag leaves its tasks intact. Dates are one field that can be a point or a span — **Duration is a mode of the date picker and a Premium feature** — with no independent start or defer date, so a "Today" filter cannot distinguish starts-today from due-today. Effort estimation exists as **"Estimated Pomo/Duration"** but is reached through the focus flow, not the date flow, and whether it is reported against actuals anywhere is undocumented. The most interesting non-obvious thing in the whole task model is **Suggested Tasks** (shipped in v8.0): it ranks candidates by neglect, sorting **Postponed Tasks** "by how many times their dates have been changed — from highest to lowest" and bucketing **Long Overdue** by creation age over 7, 14 or 30 days ([Suggested Tasks](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7401564165023727616)). That is real behavioural triage data — and TickTick uses it only for a list.

## Focus offers three repairs for lost time and hides the one that matters

The timer is two modes on one surface: **Pomodoro** (countdown, documented default 25/5) and **Stopwatch** (count-up, no limit), chosen before Start, switchable mid-flow without losing the linked task or accumulated time ([How to Start Focus?](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782010496745472)). Duration is adjustable **while running** via +/- controls on the time area, and pause is **capped at three times per session**, a 2020 change from no pause at all ([TickTick blog, 2020-05-06](https://blog.ticktick.com/2020/05/06/brand-new-focus-experience-ticktick/)). Current Pomo Settings fields, ranges, defaults, the long-break length and whether auto-start toggles still exist are all **unverified** — the live help centre documents only the ringtone field, and the field inventory in circulation comes from a 2018 blog post. The minimum duration that counts as a recorded session is likewise unknown; community folklore that short pomos are discarded has no official support.

Linking is where TickTick answers "users forget to start the timer", and it answers it by **making starting cost one gesture from wherever the user already is**: the Focus tab's own task picker, a configurable swipe action on a task row, an iOS long-press context menu, desktop right-click, the task detail "..." menu, a **habit** detail "..." menu (habits are first-class link targets), and — the only path requiring no memory at all, because the system initiates it — **"Focus" directly in the task reminder pop-up**. A separate **Timers** entity removes configuration from the critical path: named reusable presets ("work", "language learning", "bedtime meditation") each storing their own mode and duration and optionally **bound to a task or habit**, with prior "Focused Days and Total Focus" counted into them ([Timer](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781982436360192)). The record's task link is nullable — the Focus Record page filters by **"Linked Status"** — but it is load-bearing, because the By List and By Tag statistics resolve through the linked task, so an unlinked record is invisible to most of the analytics surface.

Manual entry exists and is first-class in intent: "If you need to add a focus record retroactively, you can also click '+ Add Record' directly on this page", reachable from at least four places including the Pomodoro page's "..." menu, the Focus Statistics page, the Focus Record page, and a desktop right-click on a Timer ([Focus Statistics](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781966800486400)). It appears on no Premium comparison row — though the table omits Focus entirely, so that proves nothing. **There is an unresolved conflict about what the Add Record dialog asks for.** One research pass found no source mentioning start or end time fields and concluded the flow is duration-based; against that, TickTick's own MCP surface exposes `update_focus` with the explicit constraint "**Focus duration cannot be changed**" ([TickTick MCP](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781759713968128)), which implies the record is anchored on a time window with duration frozen against it. Both readings are in the notes and neither was verified in-app; the report does not resolve it.

What is clear is that TickTick ships **three distinct affordances** for unrecorded time and prioritises them in exactly the wrong order for bat-board's problem. **Extra Time** extends a session that just ended ("if you're still working on the task, tap Extra Time"). **Edit Focus Duration** shrinks an overrun, but only on the Focus Completion page — "If you finish earlier but forget to stop the timer" — after which duration is frozen forever and the only recourse is delete-and-re-add. **Add Record** creates a session from nothing, and it is the one buried deepest, behind a "..." menu or a "+" on a statistics sub-page. The placement is itself a finding: the most discoverable backfill button sits where you go when you already suspect your numbers are wrong.

Statistics are substantial and entirely self-comparative: a trend chart by week/month/year, a per-day **Focus Timeline** showing when you focused, a **Most Focused Time** hour-of-day statistic, a **Year Grid** heat map, Day/Week/Month/Custom ranges, and distributions **By List, By Tag and By Task**. The best idea on that surface for bat-board's purposes is the calendar overlay: View Options → **Show Focus Records** draws focus blocks alongside scheduled tasks, which makes *missing* time visible as a gap and invites backfill without a nag. No export of focus data is documented anywhere; the read-only MCP `get_focuses_by_time` (capped at one month per query) is the only documented extraction path. Distraction handling is a deep stack that is almost entirely unbuildable without a mobile app: white noise (paywall status disputed between sources), three full-screen clock styles, screen-always-on, a floating window, **Flip Start** (the timer runs only face-down and auto-pauses when you pick the phone up), **Strict Mode** app blocking with an allowlist (Pomodoro-only, iOS 16+), and an iOS Live Activity. Focus time syncs out to Apple Health's **Mindfulness** category; no Screen Time integration exists. What a "give up" action does to a record is undocumented, as is force-quit behaviour.

## The Achievement Score was narrowed twice because people farmed it

TickTick's points system is a **cumulative lifetime score that can go down**, recomputed **once a day at 00:00, not in real time** ([Achievements and Statistics](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7082302534169133056)). The official definition is narrow: "Achievement values are directly related to the completion of tasks and task delays", with "Completing tasks on time will give you additional boosts" and "If tasks are left unprocessed or expire, you may lose some achievement points". **TickTick's own docs contradict each other on the input set**: the FAQ says the score "is related to your daily use of TickTick, such as creating tasks, completing tasks, focusing, and habit tracking" ([FAQ](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055792921664028672)), which is flatly incompatible with the Achievements article's "Only actions directly related to achieving goals will affect your achievement score". The history resolves which is stale rather than which is wrong: in October 2017 TickTick announced that "your achievement scores are now directly related to completed tasks and overdue tasks only" ([TickTick blog, 2017-10-18](https://blog.ticktick.com/2017/10/18/say-hi-to-ticktick-40/)). The FAQ was simply never updated. **No point values, thresholds or formulas have ever been published**, and nothing here should be used to guess one.

Levels are **12 tiers**, up from 10 in 2017, with **no published names and no thresholds**, and exactly one unlock: **exclusive themes**. The reward ladder is cosmetic end to end — no feature, storage or capability sits behind achievement level, which means the score can never create a grind-to-function dynamic. Badges are the one fully documented part: **six families × six tiers**, with inputs spanning tasks completed, habit check-ins, accumulated focus minutes, days the app was opened, friends invited, and **months of membership paid** ([Badges](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7082279841969471488)). Three of those deserve attention. The **daily caps are explicit** — "There is a daily cap of 50 badges" on task completions and "a limit of 10 check-ins per day" on habits — which is a direct admission that these counters were farmable. The **VIP family is a badge for spending money** and **Warm Heart is a badge for referrals**: both measure the company's interest, not the user's. And the tier ladders for both **Perseverance (3, 7, 21, 50, 200, 1000 days) and Self-Discipline (3, 7, 21, 100, 500, 2000 check-ins)** open with **3/7/21 — TickTick's badge thresholds embed the 21-day habit myth**, which bat-board's standing evidence rules ban outright. That ladder is the clearest single thing in this report *not* to copy.

Badges and the score are decoupled systems, and there is **no task streak anywhere in TickTick** — Perseverance counts total days the app was opened, not consecutive ones, and Self-Discipline counts total check-ins. TickTick's loss-aversion lever is the **per-task overdue deduction**, a graduated cost rather than an all-or-nothing chain reset, which is a meaningfully gentler failure mode. Its habit guidance pushes the same way, toward shrinking the goal rather than protecting a chain: "make it super simple… setting a goal of 5 words per day… reading 2 pages a day" ([Start a New Habit](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781500696363008)). One anti-gaming rule is worth copying verbatim because bat-board already shares its philosophy: shared-list actions are firewalled out of your score, so "If a task you assigned to someone else is delayed, your achievement points won't decrease. Similarly, tasks added or completed by others in a shared list won't increase your achievement points."

The most copyable metric on the whole surface is not the score at all. **Completion rate is defined as "the ratio of the tasks you actually completed today/this week/this month to the tasks you originally scheduled"** — a rate, not a volume, so it cannot be inflated by creating easy tasks, and it punishes over-planning as well as under-doing ([Achievements and Statistics](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7082302534169133056)). Two display defaults are telling about TickTick's own confidence: the Year View heatmap is **off by default** and the Summary feature is **hidden by default**, while the achievement score is always on.

On whether there is a leaderboard, the honest answer is **unresolved**. The complete Achievements article and the FAQ answer mention no rank, percentile, leaderboard or comparison, and the shared-list isolation rule pushes the opposite way; one older third-party tips article describes a Stats panel showing "how much more productive are you than the rest of the users", and App Store reviewers describe using the score competitively ([MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/tag/ticktick-tips/)). The most likely reality is a single anonymous percentile line, with no named peers and no table — but nobody on this project had app access to confirm it, and no opt-out setting is documented. Note the naming trap: TickTick does use "ranking" in Focus Statistics, where it means ranking your own lists and tags by focus time.

On criticism, the evidence is thin and conflicted and the report will not inflate it. Nearly every critical source **sells a competing gamified app**. The sharpest critique available is structural rather than emotional, and it survives the conflict of interest: the mechanics "sit alongside the task list rather than being integrated into it", and "TickTick's points and achievements are nice. They're rarely the reason someone opens the app" ([Taskoria](https://taskoria.app/blog/ticktick-alternatives-gamified)). A competitor's claim that the score "measures activity, not progress. Points for using the app, streaks for showing up" is **factually wrong about the current app** on both counts. **No verified user report of score gaming and no verified complaint about demotivation after a score drop could be found** — the three guardrails are circumstantial evidence that farming happened, and direct testimony is absent.

The behavioural literature behind all of this is more equivocal than vendor blogs suggest. Deci, Koestner & Ryan's 1999 meta-analysis of 128 experiments found **tangible, expected, contingent rewards reduce intrinsic motivation for already-interesting tasks while verbal rewards tend to increase it** ([PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)), but the scope is contested and a 2022 replication of Deci (1971) did not support the overjustification effect in that design ([APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/fulltext/2022-53480-001.pdf)). Gamification raises short-term engagement with decent evidence while **certainty for long-term maintenance is very low** ([Springer review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-026-01798-3)), and over-reliance on points, badges and leaderboards without meaningful challenge produces rapid diminishing returns ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/16/8/1200)). The streak result is the actionable one: across seven studies, Silverman & Barasch found that **intact streaks highlighted in a behavioural log increase subsequent engagement relative to broken ones, and the effect is "independent of actual past behavior" — it "depends solely on how that behavior is represented within the log"**, with the damage **amplified when people blame themselves and attenuated when the streak can be repaired** ([INSEAD](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer)). The display does the harm, not the missed day. Several widely circulated streak statistics — a "40% more effort" figure, a 2023 *Journal of Behavioral Medicine* study, a CHI 2020 "top quit reason" — appear only in vendor blogs with no retrievable primary source and were deliberately excluded.

## Reminders escalate until acted on, and never notice being ignored

TickTick's calendar is a **multi-object canvas**: one View Options menu controls whether tasks, habits, countdowns, focus records, checklist items, completed items and future recurrence cycles are drawn ([Calendar View Options](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782085826445312)). The view set is unusually large — List, Day, 3-Day, Week, Multi-Day, Multi-Week, Month, Year, Agenda, Timeline and a Split View — and is tiered as "Calendar View: Basic / Unlimited". Time blocking is gesture-rich and mature: an **Arrange tasks** drawer of undated tasks to drag onto the timeline, click or long-press-drag to create a block of any duration, drag a block edge to resize, drag across date cells for multi-day spans, desktop multi-select, and a bulk **Postpone** button that shifts several tasks forward at once ([Week View](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782149730861056)). **Nothing in six relevant help articles describes conflict detection, overlap warnings or any capacity check** — that is an argument from documented silence rather than an explicit denial, but it is consistent: TickTick optimises the *speed of placement*, never the *quality of the plan*, and its answer to an overloaded day is "shift everything right" with no notion of what should be dropped. Since **Duration is Premium**, most of TickTick's users never time-block at all; their scheduling is a due date plus a reminder, which is precisely the configuration that degenerates into noise.

The reminder surface is wide: relative or absolute reminders per task (**2 free, 5 Premium**), a global default reminder, an end-of-duration reminder, Premium checklist-item reminders, mobile-only location reminders, web-configured Premium email notifications, and a **Priority Reminder Ringtone** that gives different priorities different sounds "so you can distinguish the priority of the task through the ringtone without having to open your phone" ([Effective Reminder Feature](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055782395743567872)). That ringtone is TickTick's *only* signal-differentiation mechanism, and it is static — derived from priority, never from behaviour. The flagship is **Constant Reminder**, a Premium feature that "keeps notifying you until you take action"; on iOS 26+ in alarm mode it uses the system alarm, and TickTick states plainly: "Yes, they will always ring and vibrate. This is a system-level alarm that bypasses Silent Mode / Do Not Disturb, ensuring 100% reliable alerts" ([Constant Reminder](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7374017550633402368)). The legacy cadence is two notifications within a minute, cycling 30 seconds on and 30 off for five minutes, continuing until action is taken. Since the June release it can be enabled separately for tasks, **habits** and anniversaries.

The reminder pop-up offers **Dismiss, Start Focus, Done, Snooze**, snooze duration is configurable "in app settings", and on iOS alarm mode a power or volume press snoozes for a fixed nine minutes. And here is the finding that matters most: **nothing in TickTick's documentation indicates the app ever adapts, backs off, de-escalates or stops reminding in response to repeated snoozes or ignores.** Reminders "continue until the task is completed, postponed, or otherwise handled" — the terminating condition is user action, never elapsed time or an ignore count. TickTick's implicit model is that the alarm is right and the user is the variable, and its only lever is volume. The irony is that it **already collects the right signal**: Suggested Tasks ranks by postpone count and task age, but it is pull-based, reached by tapping a 💡 in the Today list, its only action is "Add to Today", and it changes no notification behaviour. Whether snoozing even counts as a postpone for that ranking is undocumented. There is **no push daily digest** either — TickTick's own help centre teaches users to build a morning briefing out of iOS Shortcuts, which is strong evidence that no first-party one exists. Smart Recognition parses dates, repeats, early reminders and postponed reminders from task text, resolving ambiguous times forward ("the current actual time is 4pm, when you enter 'remind me at 9pm' it will be recognised as 9pm today"), and NLP is free while AI voice capture and MCP are Premium.

Two external-sync details are worth noting because they predict the complaints. TickTick runs **two different Google mechanisms** — "Subscribe from" keeps external events as events, while "Integration with" promotes TickTick tasks to Google events and Google events to TickTick *tasks*, which is why the integration needs a configurable default tag (`GCal`) and default list to stop promoted events polluting the task space ([Google Calendar](https://help.ticktick.com/articles/7055781593733922816)). Events dated before today never sync in, and disconnecting **leaves orphans** that the docs tell you to delete "one by one". **No refresh interval is published for any provider.**

## Where the evidence stops

Four things in this report are uncertain in ways that matter, and one source was unavailable to everyone.

**Reddit was unreachable to every researcher on this project.** `reddit.com` and `old.reddit.com` are blocked to the fetch tooling, domain-scoped search is refused, and the redlib mirror returned only a loading page. Every user-sentiment claim here therefore rests on review aggregators — G2, Capterra, TrustRadius, TechRadar, AlternativeTo — plus App Store review text retrieved only through search summaries, and competitor blogs selling gamified alternatives. Those sources are mostly undated and sometimes self-contradictory within a single reviewer. **This report contains no survey of TickTick user complaints and nothing here should be presented as one.** The design gaps listed above (no monthly recurrence, no "at most" mode, no real description field, fixed sections, no freeze mechanic, mobile/desktop asymmetry) are derived from documented mechanics, not from observed user testimony. The two complaint *shapes* that review sites do show, both failures of trust, are "it didn't fire" and "it fired as an undifferentiated lump" — and notably **no source, official or user, describes anyone complaining that TickTick reminded them too little**.

The specific unresolved contradictions, carried forward rather than smoothed over: **TickTick's own docs disagree about what earns Achievement Score** (the Achievements article says task completion and delay only; the FAQ adds creating tasks, focusing and habit tracking — the 2017 changelog suggests the FAQ is stale, but TickTick has not said so). **The default priority-to-quadrant mapping is published for exactly one of four quadrants.** **The Add Record field list is disputed**: one search pass found no mention of start/end time fields and inferred a duration-only dialog, while `update_focus`'s "Focus duration cannot be changed" implies a stored time window. **The habit streak increment rule is self-contradictory in TickTick's blog** — per check-in or per successful week, for a weekly-count habit. Smaller conflicts: whether white noise is paywalled (two reviews disagree), whether calendar subscription is Premium (old App Store copy versus the current table), the Premium price ($35.99 in third-party reviews versus **$49.99/yr on the current upgrade page**), and per-task attachment limits quoted as both 20 and 39 within the same help corpus.

## What bat-board should build, in priority order

The brief's two stated problems are not symmetrical. **Forgetting to start the timer is a capture problem with a cheap, well-understood fix.** **Reminders decaying into noise is a modelling problem that TickTick has not solved and leaves wide open** — it collects ignore signals and spends them on a list. The ordering below reflects that: the reminder work is the one place where bat-board can be straightforwardly better than the product it is studying, not merely cheaper.

| # | Change | Area | Addresses | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reminder state machine with real back-off and a re-commit prompt | Reminders | Problem (b) | Medium |
| 2 | Inline "Start focus" button on every Alfred reminder | Focus capture | Problem (a) | Small |
| 3 | Retrospective gap prompt from the cron tick | Focus capture | Problem (a) | Medium |
| 4 | Day-of-week and times-per-week habit schedules | Habits | Scheduling | Medium |
| 5 | Skip state with its own row, excluded from the denominator | Habits | Scheduling | Small |
| 6 | Named focus presets bound to a mission or habit | Focus capture | Problem (a) | Small |
| 7 | Completion rate as the headline motivation number | Motivation | Motivation | Small |
| 8 | Quantified habit goals (boolean/numeric, goal/step/unit) | Habits | Scheduling | Medium |
| 9 | Habit archiving, and streaks derived server-side from the log | Habits | Integrity | Small |
| 10 | Daily digest replacing per-item pings; one morning message | Reminders | Problem (b) | Small |

**1. Give reminders a state machine that notices being ignored.** This is the single highest-value thing in the report, because it is the gap TickTick leaves open while holding all the data. Add a `reminder_events` table — reminder id, fired_at, channel, acknowledged_at, outcome (`done` / `snoozed` / `rescheduled` / `ignored`) — and have the cron tick write a row on every send and close it on every Telegram callback. Then make the send decision a function of that history rather than of the clock alone. After **three consecutive unacknowledged fires**, stop re-sending the reminder text at all and have Alfred send one re-commit message instead: *"Medicine at 09:00 has fired eleven times with no reply. Keep it, move it, change what it says, or drop it?"* with four inline buttons. A month of identical ignored pings should be structurally impossible: the invariant to enforce is that **bat-board never sends the same unchanged reminder text more than three times in a row**. This is also where Suggested Tasks' honest framing belongs — "the ones you still want to pursue" — applied to reminders rather than to tasks. Do **not** copy Constant Reminder or anything resembling DND bypass; that escalation philosophy is what produced the owner's ignored-medicine month, and it is unbuildable over Telegram anyway.

**2. Put a "Start focus" button on every reminder Alfred sends.** TickTick has at least six focus launch points, and exactly one requires no memory from the user: the reminder pop-up, because the system initiates it. Telegram inline keyboards make this nearly free — a `Start focus` callback on every mission or habit reminder that creates a running session already linked to the right row. This is the cheapest meaningful fix for problem (a), and it should ship before anything else on the focus side.

**3. Have the cron tick ask about gaps it can see.** The deeper version of problem (a) is that the owner works without starting anything, so the board records nothing and there is no session to repair. Two mechanisms close that. First, build the **focus timeline strip** TickTick draws with "Show Focus Records": a day-width band in the React UI showing logged blocks against the clock, so unlogged time appears as a visible hole. Passive visibility invites backfill without a nag. Second, make Alfred act on those holes from `services/scheduler.py` — detect a span over, say, ninety minutes inside the user's declared working window with no focus record and no calendar event, and send one message at a natural boundary: *"Nothing logged between 14:00 and 16:15. Was that the Q3 migration? [Yes, log 2h15] [Something else] [Not working]"*. One tap writes a `logged_manually` record with the mission link attached. Keep this strictly rate-limited — at most one such prompt per day, suppressed entirely if the previous one was ignored, so it cannot itself become recommendation 1's problem.

Keep the three repair affordances distinct, as TickTick does, and **do not bury the backfill button** — TickTick's most discoverable Add Record entry point is a "+" on a statistics sub-page, found only by users who already suspect their numbers are wrong. Bat-board's mission-row +15m/+30m/+1h taps are already the Extra Time equivalent and are well placed; what is missing is a correction at session end (TickTick's Edit Focus Duration, available only on the completion screen) and a genuinely visible "log past session" entry point on the mission row itself, not only in a focus panel. On immutability, copy TickTick's split exactly: **freeze the duration once a record is written and keep the link and note editable**, which is what `update_focus`'s "Focus duration cannot be changed" enforces. The existing rule that `logged_manually` records are excluded from the median session length is right and should be defended — TickTick's By List and By Tag breakdowns resolve through the linked task, so an unlinked record is nearly worthless, which argues for pushing the user to attach a mission on every manual add while still keeping that record out of the median.

**4. Replace the three-value frequency enum with a real schedule.** Today a weekly habit is "any one check-in in a Mon–Sun bucket", which is the `TT_TIMES` model without the count. Migration 023 (additive, nullable, reversible) should add `schedule_kind` (`daily` / `weekly_count` / `interval`), `byday` (a CSV or JSON array of weekday codes), `times_per_period` (int), and `interval_days` (int), with existing rows mapping to the current behaviour so nobody breaks. Then adopt TickTick's genuinely good decision: **evaluate adherence and streaks against the frequency target, not against consecutive calendar days**, which is what bat-board's 14-day recent-adherence measure already half does. A day the schedule does not call for must not count against anything. Skip monthly-by-date and yearly for now — they are the two things TickTick lacks and users ask for, but they are not where bat-board's leverage is. Do **not** copy fixed morning/afternoon/evening sections; if sections arrive, let users name them.

**5. Add an explicit skip, stored as its own row.** Bat-board has a one-miss-per-run grace allowance, which is a silent repair. TickTick has `exDates` at the habit level and once had a "Mark as Unachieved" state with time and reason, and the current app documents neither clearly. The evidence-aligned design is a `habit_skips` row carrying the date and an optional reason, where **a skipped scheduled day is removed from the adherence denominator rather than counted as a miss**. That gives the "repairable" moderator Silverman & Barasch identified — the one thing shown to attenuate the harm of a break — without a freeze gimmick, without a grace day that fires automatically behind the user's back, and without any dependence on the chain metaphors the project bans. Pair it with the honest display rule that follows from "the effect depends solely on how that behavior is represented within the log": keep measuring adherence, and **stop rendering a broken chain as a loss**. Also allow unlimited-horizon backfill, keep `logged_at` distinct from the check-in date so backfills are auditable, and decide explicitly — unlike TickTick, where it is unverified — that a backfilled day **does** repair adherence.

**6. Add named focus presets bound to a mission or habit.** TickTick's Timers remove configuration from the critical path: a saved "Reading / stopwatch" or "Deep work / 50m" that starts with one tap and arrives pre-linked. In bat-board this is a small table plus a Telegram command (`/focus reading`) and a row of buttons on the focus panel. Combined with recommendation 2, starting a session and deciding what it is for become the same action.

**7. Make completion rate the headline number, and leave the points system narrow.** TickTick's completion rate — completed divided by **originally scheduled** — is the most honest metric in the entire product: a rate rather than a volume, uninflatable by creating easy work, and it penalises over-planning as well as under-doing. Bat-board should compute and show it per week. On points, the existing design is already most of the way to TickTick's hard-won position: narrow inputs, work earning nothing, a cap built into `5 + min(recent adherence, 10)`. Three things to add or avoid. Add a **daily cap on any farmable event**, which is exactly what TickTick's "daily cap of 50" completions and "limit of 10 check-ins per day" are for. Keep rewards **cosmetic** — TickTick's twelve levels unlock nothing but themes, which is precisely why its score is described as weak-but-harmless rather than corrosive, and the Deci meta-analysis warns specifically about rewards that are tangible, expected and contingent. And **do not build a leaderboard**: TickTick's social comparison is at most an anonymous percentile, it firewalls other users' actions out of your score, and the same firewall rule should apply if bat-board ever shares surfaces between users. If badges arrive, **do not copy the 3/7/21 ladder** that TickTick uses for both Perseverance and Self-Discipline — it embeds the 21-day myth this codebase bans — and do not copy VIP (a badge for paying) or Warm Heart (a badge for referrals). A badge for *app opens*, as Perseverance is, rewards nothing but vanity engagement. Finally, resist an overdue deduction: TickTick's is graduated and gentler than a chain reset, but bat-board already decided that tying habit reward to the streak punished a missed day twice over, and a points deduction is the same mistake wearing different clothes. Let the completion rate fall on its own.

**8–10, the remainder.** Quantified goals are worth having and cheap to model TickTick's way — `goal`, `step` and `unit` as floats, boolean or numeric type, partial progress stored on the row with no credit until `value >= goal` — but note that bat-board's `minimum_viable` two-minute version already implements the better half of the same idea, which is shrinking the goal rather than tracking fractions, and that is also what TickTick's own habit guidance recommends ("5 words per day", "2 pages a day"). Add archiving as `status` plus `archived_at` with history preserved and `best_streak` untouched, and **never offer a delete that takes the check-in log with it** the way TickTick's does. While you are in that code, move the streak and adherence computation server-side, derived from the check-in log on read or at tick: TickTick's `currentStreak` is a denormalised integer written by the client, which is exactly how counters drift across devices, offline edits and backfills. And replace per-item reminder pings with **one morning digest** from Alfred plus escalation only for genuinely time-critical items — TickTick has no push daily plan at all and teaches users to build one in Shortcuts, pushing per-task alarms while making the daily overview a pull. For a user drowning in alerts that is backwards, and Telegram makes the correct version trivial. When a reminder does fire, differentiate the signal: TickTick's priority ringtone is its only such mechanism and it is static, while Alfred can vary message shape by state and by how many times this reminder has already gone unanswered.

## Conclusion

TickTick's mechanics divide cleanly into two kinds, and the division is more useful than any individual feature. The first kind reduces the cost of an action — six focus launch points, saved Timers, an Arrange-tasks drawer, drag-to-resize blocks, a bulk Postpone button, swipe check-ins, unlimited backfill. TickTick is excellent at this, and most of it is cheap to copy or already present in bat-board. The second kind would require the product to *model* the user: to notice that a reminder has been ignored eleven times, that a day is over-committed, that a task postponed six times is not a scheduling problem. TickTick collects the data for all three — postpone counts, task age, block overlaps, acknowledgement state — and converts none of it into a decision. It ships a bulk Postpone with no notion of what should be dropped, a Suggested Tasks panel that ranks neglect and changes nothing, and an alarm that bypasses Do Not Disturb to guarantee delivery of a message its own data shows the user is not reading. That is the open flank, and it is the owner's problem (b) stated in TickTick's architecture.

The gamification history is the other lesson, and it runs the opposite way from most product instincts. TickTick's score got *narrower* over time — inputs cut to completion and delay, daily caps added to badges, other people's work firewalled out — and its rewards stayed purely cosmetic, updated nightly in batch rather than instantly. The cost is the complaint competitors make: nobody opens the app for it. The benefit is that the score is a mirror rather than a lever, which is the only configuration the evidence supports, given that the undermining effect is specifically about rewards that are tangible, expected and contingent, and that long-term maintenance evidence for gamification is very low. Read alongside Silverman & Barasch's finding that a streak's effect depends on how behaviour is *represented* rather than on the behaviour itself, the defensible position for bat-board is already close to the one it holds: measure adherence, make misses repairable, keep the rewards informational and cosmetic, cap anything farmable, and never render a broken chain. The work left is not to add motivation mechanics. It is to make the board see time the owner actually spent, and to stop Alfred saying the same thing into a void.
